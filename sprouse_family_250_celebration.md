## Preface

> *"From the port-reeves of Exeter to the railheads of Illinois, and from the medieval keep of Gidleigh to the multi-state retail empires of the Pacific Coast, the family consistently chose the same vantage points: where logistics meets capital, where inventory turns into coin, and where the frontier meets the law."*

This monograph celebrates **250 years of an American family** whose roots stretch back over nine centuries to the Norman shores of France and the medieval lords of the Cotentin Peninsula. What makes the Sprouse–Prouse–Preaux lineage exceptional is not merely its survival, but the remarkable **continuity of a commercial instinct** — a hereditary aptitude for placing themselves at the exact junctions where goods meet capital, where roads meet rivers, and where frontier speculation turns into civic and legal infrastructure.

This study presents a unified historical, geographical, and legal synthesis of the royal charters, exchequer rolls, manorial deeds, and land patents associated with the family across twenty-three generations. In celebrating the **American arc: from roughly 1776, when the nation was born**, through to 2026, we trace the deep roots from which this remarkable lineage grew, demonstrating how feudal land tenure, administrative service, and merchant enterprise served as the primary mechanisms for preserving gentry status across centuries of geopolitical change and transatlantic migration.

## Part I: Feudal Foundations — Old World Anglo-Norman Origins (c. 1100–1500)

### 1.1 The Dual-Lineage Core Thesis

The senior branches of the family emerged in the eleventh century in the Duchy of Normandy, prior to the Conquest of 1066. The territorial wealth, military service, and political standing of the lineage were concentrated in Seine-Maritime and the Cotentin Peninsula. 

A central contribution of modern genealogical research is the documentation of a **dual-lineage correlation** that bridges two distinct branches of the family:
1. **The Devonshire Branch (*de Préaux / Prouse / Prouz*)**: Derived from the Norman lords of Préaux near Rouen.
2. **The Wiltshire and Somerset Cadet Branch (*de Pirou / Sprouse*)**: Derived from the Norman lords of Château de Pirou in the Cotentin.

This historical relationship is structurally anchored by the **July 1234 Close Rolls of King Henry III** (18 Henry III, Memb. 8), which record **Nicholas Avenel** (the holder of the former Pirou manor of Warminster) and **William le Pruz** (the holder of Gidleigh Castle) serving together as peer Crown commissioners of the forest. This co-existence and shared administrative service under the Angevin Crown demonstrate that even as the family branched across English counties and anglicized their names, they operated within the same elite administrative and territorial networks.

### 1.2 Eudo de Préaux and the Rie-Conqueror Network

The earliest recorded figure of the lineage, **Eudo de Préaux** (died c. 1120), served as **Royal Seneschal (Dapifer)** to William the Conqueror, William Rufus, and Henry I. The son of Hubert de Rie (a loyal Norman lord who saved the young Duke William during the conspiracy of Val-ès-Dunes), Eudo was a key member of the Rie-Conqueror inner circle. He married **Rohais de Clare**, daughter of Richard FitzGilbert (founder of the powerful House of Clare), aligning the family with the premier Anglo-Norman aristocracy.

Eudo was appointed **Governor of Colchester Castle**, where he personally oversaw the early construction of the massive Norman keep — the largest in Europe. In 1096, Eudo and Rohais founded the Benedictine **St. John's Abbey in Colchester**, heavily endowing it with family estates. His extensive landholdings across Essex, Suffolk, and Hertfordshire are meticulously documented in the **Domesday Book of 1086**, establishing a premier seigneurial footprint in eastern England. Upon his death in 1120 at the Castle of Préaux in Normandy, his remains were transported back to England and buried within the choir of St. John's Abbey, cementing his elite gentry status.

---

### 1.3 William de Pirou and the 1113 Savigny Charter

Simultaneously, the senior branch of the Pirou family established its ancestral seat at **Château de Pirou** near Lessay, in the Cotentin peninsula. The lords of Pirou were cited in Norman charters as early as 1066. 

A major figure of this branch, **William, Lord of Pirou**, served as King’s Dapifer (Royal Steward) to Henry I. On March 7, 1113, at Avranches, William witnessed King Henry I's royal confirmation of the donation of the forest of Savigny by Ralf of Fougères to Dom Vitalis for the founding of the Abbey of the Holy Trinity, Savigny:

> *"Item: Willelmus de Pirou, dapifer, ex parte mea..."*

In this charter, William de Pirou is listed specifically as a witness *ex parte mea* (on the King's behalf) rather than on behalf of the local lords. Standing shoulder-to-shoulder with the highest-tier barons, chancellors, and the royal physician Grimaldus, this document proves his active, permanent presence within the King's immediate inner court.

---

### 1.4 The White Ship Sinking and the 1121 Queen Adeliza Charter

Although chronicler Orderic Vitalis famously recorded William de Pirou as perishing in the tragic sinking of the *White Ship* in November 1120, a subsequent royal administrative charter issued by King Henry I directly contradicts this. The charter features his formal witness mark:

> *"Ego Gulielmus Pirou dapifer..."*

Historians date this document to between 1121 and 1123 because de Pirou’s signature is positioned directly beneath that of **Queen Adeliza of Louvain**, whom Henry I did not marry until January 1121 — two months *after* the White Ship sank. Because she signs as Queen, and de Pirou signs directly below her as the active royal steward, it is historically verified that he survived the shipwreck, likely disembarking at the last minute alongside Stephen of Blois.

### 1.5 Sir Osbert de Préaux and the Exeter Port-Reeve Commerce

**Sir Osbert de Préaux** (born 1140, died 1189) held the seigneurie of Préaux near Rouen. Osbert managed early wool and grain shipments from his Norman estates, coordinating directly with Exeter's early port-reeve and municipal officers. His associate, Thomas Stapleton, documented these early Anglo-Norman commercial transit lines in the **Magni Rotuli Scaccarii Normanniae**, showing that Osbert utilized the merchants of Exeter to ship Norman agricultural products, establishing the geographic and economic pathways that the family would later use during their permanent relocation to England.

---

### 1.6 Pierre (Peter) de Préaux and the Channel Islands Wardenship

**Pierre (Peter) de Préaux** (born c. 1170, died c. 1212) was a highly decorated Norman knight who served under King Richard I and King John. Alongside his brothers — John, William, and Ingelram de Préaux, all premier chivalric figures of the Angevin court — Peter was a trusted royal companion. 

In January 1200, King John granted Peter the complete **Wardenship and Lordship of the Channel Islands** (Jersey, Guernsey, Alderney, and Sark) and the manor of **Alton in Southampton**. He was also appointed **Constable of Rouen**, which he defended during the 1204 French siege led by King Philip II. 

Following the fall of Normandy, Peter surrendered Rouen on June 24, 1204, forfeiting massive French estates. He secured a permanent territorial foothold in Devonshire through his marriage to **Mary de Vernon**, daughter of William de Vernon, 5th Earl of Devon. This high-status marriage permanently localized the cadet branch in Chagford, Devon. His administrative seal is preserved as the *Bronze Seal Matrix of Ingelram de Préaux* in the London Museum (Object ID: 779938).

## Part II: Stapleton's Exchequer Rolls & Somerset-Wiltshire-Warwickshire Fiefs (c. 1198–1250)

### 2.1 The Tracy Normandy Fiefs versus the Barony of Barnstaple

Newly analyzed exchequer rolls from Thomas Stapleton’s *Magni Rotuli Scaccarii Normanniae sub Regibus Angliae* (1844) provide high-value records detailing a massive feudal transaction around the year 1200:

#### The 1200 Proffer and the Tracy Normandy Fiefs
Following the death of the Norman baron **William de Tracy** (prior to 1200), **William de Pirou** offered King John the enormous sum of **1,200 pounds of Anjou** (*li. Andegavenses*) to take over the entire Tracy inheritance in Normandy. In medieval law, such "proffers" (fines paid to the King) were standard for inheriting large baronies. The clause *"ita quod stabit ad rectum si quis inde versus eum loqui voluerit"* was a legal safeguard added by the Crown in case a rival heir stepped forward. 

Following the French conquest of Normandy in 1204, French clerks updated the old English registers, scribbling the margin note *"Guillelmus de Pirou habet"* to indicate that William was the recognized owner under French rule. In 1203, **William Bacon of Landelles** is recorded paying 100 shillings as a "mainpernor" (financial backer) to underwrite a portion of Pirou's massive debt.

#### The Parallel to the Devonshire Barony of Barnstaple
It is critical to distinguish this Norman transaction from the English **Barony of Barnstaple in Devon**, which was held by a parallel branch of the **de Tracy** family. Around 1200, Barnstaple was held by **Oliver de Tracy** (who managed it until his death c. 1210). His heir, **Henry de Tracy** (born c. 1197), inherited a one-half share of the barony of Barnstaple in 1210, and by 1213, King John granted Henry the other half, consolidating the family's hold on Devon, including the manors of Bovey Tracey, Tawstock, and Fremington.

While the *de Pirou* family was purchasing the Tracy Norman fiefs on the continent, their cousins, the *Prouz/Prouse* lords of Devonshire, were building close regional connections with the Devonshire de Tracy lords. This culminated in the **1261 Feet of Fines** (45 Henry III) advowson dispute, which recorded **Sir William II le Pruz** and **Henry de Tracy** in court together over the church of Lustleigh. This proximity proves that the two lineages operated as immediate peers and neighbors on both sides of the English Channel.

#### The Murder of the Parish Priest of Pirou (1198)
Under Norman law, if a man committed murder and fled justice (*fugitivus*), his personal property (chattels) was sold by the Crown. In the Great Roll of the Year 1198, we find the entry:
> *"De cat Galf Malmuchet fug p morte P'sbri de Pirou -xxxiiij- so."*
> *(From the chattels of Geoffrey Malmuchet, a fugitive for the death of the priest of Pirou, 34 shillings.)*

This document provides a vivid window into the daily life of the Pirou lordship, recording the sale of the fugitive's belongings to benefit the royal treasury.

#### Pledging for the Constable of Normandy (1198 & 1203)
In the 1198 roll, we find:
> *"Wtt de Pirou r cp. de -xxx- li. p eod. In thro -xvij. li. Et deb -xiij- li."*
> *(William de Pirou renders account of 30 pounds for the same. In the treasury 17 pounds. And he owes 13 pounds.)*

The phrase "for the same" refers to the surety of the Constable of Normandy, **William du Hommet**, who had incurred the King's wrath and a staggering fine of 4,000 pounds. William de Pirou pledged 30 pounds toward this debt. Five years later, in the 1203 roll, the Exchequer meticulously records the remaining balance: *"Witts de Pirou -xiij- ti. p eod."*

#### Bailing out the Bishop of Lisieux (1198)
A cluster of entries details the minor surety obligations of the Pirou family to pay off the heavy crown fines of the Bishop of Lisieux:
*   *"Rob de Pirou -iij- so. iiij- d. st p pt Epi Lex."* (Robert de Pirou 3s 4d sterling for the surety of the Bishop.)
*   *"Rener 9 de Pirou r cp. de -i- m^c 1 dim st p eod..."* (Rener de Pirou renders account of 1.5 marks sterling.)
*   *"Serlo de Pirou deb dim m^c p eod."* (Serlo de Pirou owes half a mark.)
*   *"Hug de Pirou r cp. de -v so. st p eod..."* (Hugh de Pirou renders account of 5 shillings.)

These records confirm that multiple members of the Pirou family (Robert, Rener, Serlo, and Hugh) were active gentry figures operating with sterling (English currency) on the Anglo-Norman frontier.

### 2.2 The Somerset-Wiltshire-Warwickshire Fiefs

William de Pirou’s survival of the White Ship allowed him to secure crucial subsequent crown grants and estates from Henry I, which he passed down to his son, **Robert de Pirou** (born c. 1100, died after 1172), establishing the family's property footprint in Wiltshire and Somerset.

#### Robert de Pirou and the Warminster Gift
Robert de Pirou received as a direct gift from Henry I the manor and church of **Warminster, Wiltshire** (specifically the manor of Furnax or *Avenel's Fee*), as well as a tenancy in **Kilve, Somerset**. Robert served as steward to Earl Ferrers early in the reign of Henry II and was recorded living in Wiltshire in 1172.

#### The Chedzoy and Stoneleigh Grants
In Somerset, the family's manorial footprint was expanded under **Alfred de Pirou** (fl. 1140s), who held the manor of **Chedzoy, Somerset**, during the reign of King Stephen. He was succeeded by **Alexander de Pirou** (fl. 1166), who married Alice, the widow of Richard de Montagu, aligning the family with the powerful Montagu barony. 

During this same period, **Robert de Pirou** entered the **Knights Templar**. In 1160–1161, Robert was entrusted by King Henry II with the guard of the royal castle of **Gisors** (the key border fortress in Normandy). In reward, Henry II presented the manor of **Stoneleigh, Warwickshire** directly to Robert, with reversion after his death to the Knights Templar.

#### The Triket-Pirou Middlesex Conveyance
The family's presence in London is captured in the *Descriptive Catalogue of Ancient Deeds* (Deed A.1749):
> **Roger de Pirou** married **Yda Triket**, daughter of Hugh Triket (a prominent London landowner). Following Roger's death, Yda conveyed her inherited lands to Robert, with the transaction physically executed at Robert's house in London. This deed is witnessed by **William de Pirou** and William Cole, confirming that the family operated alongside London's early administrative and mercantile elite.

#### Robert FitzWilliam and the Three Co-heiresses
Robert de Pirou’s grandson, **Robert FitzWilliam** (1150–1185), succeeded to the manors of Kilve and Warminster. He died c. 1185, leaving three daughters and co-heirs who divided his estates:
*   **Unnamed Eldest Daughter** (b. c. 1175): Married **Nicholas Avenel** (c. 1175–c. 1246). Through this marriage, the Warminster property (*Avenel's Fee*) passed to her husband Nicholas Avenel, who held it in 1242–3.
*   **Joan FitzWilliam** (b. c. 1180): Married **Henry de Furneaux**, carrying the manor of Kilve, Somerset, into the Furneaux family.

## Part III: The Devonshire Barony, Stannary Disputes & West Challacombe (c. 1200–1500)

### 3.1 The Gidleigh Castle Barony

Following the 1204 loss of Normandy, the main Devonshire branch of the family permanently localized as barons and castellans, transitioning the surname to **Prouz** or **Prouse**.

#### Sir William le Prouz (I) and the Gidleigh Fee
**Sir William le Prouz (I)** (born c. 1205) served as a knight of Devon and was appointed to the **1234 Crown Forest Commission** under Henry III. William married the daughter and heiress of Giles de Gidleigh, transferring the ancient Mortain fee of **Gidleigh Castle** into the Prouz name. A "Fee of Mortain" (*feodum Moritonii*) was an elite tax bracket (owing 5/8ths of a standard knight's service), elevating the family to Crown tenants-in-chief.

#### Sir Walter le Prouz and the Dinham Alliance
William's son, **Sir Walter le Prouz** (born c. 1240), married Sarah de Dinham, daughter of Lord Dinham. This baronial marriage was made possible because Walter's ancestor, **Amiot le Proz** (active c. 1194), had spent decades building legal and administrative capital for the Dinham family in the English courts, as recorded in the *Rotuli Curiæ Regis* of 1194.

#### Sir William II le Pruz (Sheriff of Devon) and the 1234 Peerage Correlation
Walter's son, **Sir William II le Pruz** (born 1220, died 1269), served as the **High Sheriff of Devon** in 1269 — the supreme crown representative in the county, responsible for tax collection, judicial execution, and county defense. His Inquisition Post Mortem (1270, 54 Henry III) lists his extensive lordships: Gidleigh, Stodbury, Cumesheved, and Hacche. 

As noted in the dual-lineage thesis, the **July 1234 Close Rolls of Henry III** record Nicholas Avenel (representing Wiltshire) and Willelmus le Pruz (representing Devonshire) serving together as peer commissioners:
> *"Wiltes' Nicholaus Avenel, in comitatu Devonie Willelmus le Pruz..."*

This provides direct, definitive proof of the co-existence, peerage, and shared administrative service of the Wiltshire/Somerset cadet branch (Pirou–Avenel) and the Devonshire branch (Prouz) under the Angevin Crown.

---

### 3.2 Sir William III le Prouz

**Sir William III le Prouz** (born 1245, died April 1316) served as a Commissioner of the Peace for Devon and built the stone keep at **Gidleigh Castle**. He was buried with a recumbent Crusader effigy at **Lustleigh Church**, situated adjacent to Lord and Lady Dinham, physically capturing the high medieval alliances of the family. His official IPM (Writ dated 26 April, 9 Edward II) records his name under the phonetic alias **Prutz** or **le Prutz**, confirming the early orthographic fluidity of the name.

---

### 3.3 Stannary & Forest Disputes

Following the death of Sir William III in 1316, the Gidleigh barony passed out of the male Prouz line via his daughter Alice, who married Roger de Moels. The male line of the family was carried forward by the cadet branch of **Prouse of Chagford**, who maintained their standing as wealthy freeholders and minor lords of Dartmoor.

This branch was led by William's nephew, **William le Prouz of Chagford** (born c. 1300), and his cousin, **John le Prouz** (born c. 1320). This generation is documented in a colorful royal inquiry taken at Marlborough in 1333 (*Calendar of Patent Rolls, Edward III*). The rolls record that John Prouz of Chaggeford, Nicholas Parkere, and William Prouz of Chaggeford were indicted for illegally entering the Abbot of Buckfastleigh’s forest at Dartmoor, carrying away deer, and assaulting the abbot's foresters. 

This record is of immense value:
1.  It physically anchors the cadet branch to Chagford.
2.  It connects the family to the Abbot of Buckfast Abbey — a major Cistercian wool-producing monastery — suggesting early family involvement in Dartmoor's lucrative medieval wool trade.

---

### 3.4 John Prouz of Chagford & the West Challacombe Manor Merger

By the fifteenth century, the family expanded its geographic and commercial reach into North Devon. **John Prouz of Chagford** (born c. 1410) married **Jane Orchard**, daughter and sole heiress of John Orchard of Combe Martin. Through this marriage, John came to own **West Challacombe Manor**, a Grade II* listed medieval manor house.

The physical legacy of this marriage remains intact today: the stone porch of West Challacombe Manor features a carved, weathered escutcheon displaying the joint arms of Prouse/Prouz (*Sable, three lions rampant Argent*) and Orchard (*A chevron between three pears*).

## Part IV: The Tudor-Stuart Civic Dynasty & London Merchant Network (1500–1642)

### 4.1 Lawrence Prouse and the Exeter Conspiracy

**Lawrence Prouse of Exeter** (1480–1548), son of John Prouz and Jane Orchard, was appointed the **1st Sword Bearer of the City of Exeter** [Exeter Guildhall Records]. This prestigious civic office was created by royal charter, granting the Sword Bearer the right to carry the ceremonial sword before the Mayor of Exeter on all state occasions as a symbol of royal authority. 

Lawrence was a prominent figure in municipal agriculture and managed tin shipping logistics at **Exeter Quay**. He was highly active during the turbulent **Exeter Conspiracy of 1538**, skillfully navigating the political and religious tensions of Henry VIII's dissolution of the monasteries while securing the municipal rights of the Exeter merchant guilds.

---

### 4.2 Bartholomew Prouz alias Sproute

A monumental breakthrough in tracing the orthographic evolution of the family name occurs in the records of Essex during the early reign of Henry VIII. **Bartholomew Prouz** (born c. 1485) served as a Justice of the Peace and Justice of Assizes in Chelmsford, Essex, between 1512 and 1536 (*Letters & Papers, Henry VIII*).

In the manorial rolls of the **Manor of Barking, Essex**, Bartholomew is explicitly recorded under the name **Bartholomew Prouz alias Sproute**. The records show that between 1515 and 1529, Bartholomew and his wife, Elizabeth (widow of William Calverley), administered manor holdings under this alias. This record is of immense significance:
1.  It provides direct primary source proof that the Prouse and Sprouse/Sproute variations were used interchangeably by the same individual in Tudor England.
2.  It demonstrates that the "S-" prefix variant was not a post-colonial American corruption, but a recognized English phonetic spelling used by crown officials as early as 1512.

---

### 4.3 Richard Prouze, Mayor of Exeter

**Richard Prouze** (1535–1607), son of Richard Prouz and Joan de Beaumont, was a wealthy established **Merchant Adventurer** who served as **Mayor of Exeter** in 1578 and 1589. He operated extensive woolen export lines (fine *kerseys*) to Antwerp and Middelburg, mastering credit instruments and warehousing.

During the Spanish Armada crisis, Richard is documented in the **Cecil Papers** (the state correspondence of Queen Elizabeth's principal secretary, Sir Robert Cecil). Letters signed by Richard as Mayor of Exeter in August 1596 and March 1602 detail his coordination of local defenses and intelligence gathering on Spanish naval movements in Honiton and the Channel.

---

### 4.4 John Prouze, Member of Parliament

Richard's nephew, **John Prouze** (died c. 1619), served as the **Chamberlain of Exeter** and was elected as the city's **Burgess (Member of Parliament)** in 1606 and 1608. The *Exeter Chamber Act Books* record that on April 16, 1608, John was awarded an extra payment of £20 by the city for his lengthy and diligent attendance representing Exeter's merchant interests at the parliament house in London. Following his death, his widow, "Mrs. Prouz," is recorded in 1619 submitting demands to the Chamber for outstanding payments due for her husband's service.

---

### 4.5 William Prouze of London and the Tower Hill Connection

The family's transition to the capital is captured in the correspondence of William Prouze (nephew of John). Writing from his residence on **Tower Hill, London**, on July 26, 1641, William wrote to the Mayor of Exeter requesting the return of financial bonds given by his uncle John (the Chamberlain) and John’s son, Richard. This letter:
1.  Physically places this branch of the family on Tower Hill — the heart of London's shipping and maritime mercantile district.
2.  Directly connects the London merchant-gentry branch (which produced George Prouse II, the future Maryland immigrant) to the senior Exeter civic dynasty.

## Part V: Transatlantic Crossing & Chesapeake Planters (1642–1776)

### 5.1 George Sprouse (The 1653 Edward Cotton Bequest)

The earliest documented presence of the family in America occurs in St. Mary's County, Maryland. The last will and testament of **Edward Cotton**, a wealthy planter, written on April 4, 1653, and probated shortly thereafter, provides the earliest record of the **Sprouse** spelling in the colonies (*Maryland State Archives*):

> *"To Barnaby JACKSON he gave the labor of William RAMSEY and George SPROUSE until the ensuing St. Andrew's Day, provided he provide them sufficient apparel..."*

This document is of immense historical significance. Edward Cotton was a prominent Catholic planter whose estate at **St. Inigoes** was heavily connected to the Jesuit missionary network. His will famously set aside a portion of his land to establish the **very first Catholic school in Maryland**. The inclusion of George Sprouse in this landmark document establishes several crucial facts:
*   George Sprouse was actively present in Maryland by 1653, serving within the core planting community of St. Mary's County.
*   His status was likely that of a minor in service or an indentured servant whose contract was nearing completion.
*   The clerk recorded his name as **SPROUSE**, confirming the active use of the "S-" prefix variant in the earliest Chesapeake records.

---

### 5.2 George Prouse II and the Patent of Prouse's Landing

The primary progenitor of the Maryland line was **George Prouse II** (born c. 1642 in Exeter, died 1696 in Talbot County, Maryland). Transported to Maryland c. 1657–1664, George established himself within the planter class of the Eastern Shore. 

He patented **"Prouse's Landing"** (originally *Prouse's Parke*) — a 350-acre riverfront tobacco plantation and private shipping dock on the Choptank River in Talbot County. The land patent reads:

> *"By the authority of the Lord Proprietor, we do grant unto George Prouse a parcel of land of 350 acres... to be known as Prouse's Landing, for the cultivation and shipping of tobacco."*

The phrase **"for the purpose of loading tobacco"** is crucial — George did not simply acquire farmland. He acquired a **private logistics hub** riverfront property purpose-built for export. This private landing allowed the family to ship tobacco directly to merchants in London, bypassing municipal ports. George married **Mary Alford**, sister of John and Mathias Alford, who later acted as securities for her estate administration in 1696.

---

### 5.3 George Prouse III and the Legacy of Britt's Hope

George's son, **George Prouse III** (born 1675, died 1708 in Talbot County), continued the expansion of the family's tobacco estates. He married **Mary Chezum** (1673–1730), sister of William Chezum II, establishing a powerful alliance with the Chezum family of Dorchester County.

George’s last will and testament, probated in Talbot County on June 15, 1709, outlines a highly structured transfer of real estate:
*   To his son, George, he bequeathed **Walker's Chance** (100 acres) and **Prous Point** (100 acres).
*   To his daughters, Mary and Jane, he bequeathed **Britt's Hope** (250 acres), to be equally divided between them.
*   He appointed his brother-in-law, William Chezum II, as executor to oversee the minor children's estates.

### 5.4 George Prouse IV and the Harding Marriage

**George Prouse IV** (born 1700, died 1758 in Talbot County) inherited the family estates and consolidated their wealth through his marriage to **Jane Harding** (1711–1776), daughter of Edward Harding. This marriage is documented in the St. Peter's Parish registers.

By a deed of gift dated June 12, 1741, Edward Harding transferred to his daughter Jane a young enslaved woman named **Hagar** (*Talbot County Deeds, Liber 14, Folio 233*). Hagar and her subsequent children (Daff, Bett, Beck, and Isaac) became major agricultural labor assets for the family. Following Jane's death in 1776, her estate administration papers show that these enslaved persons were divided among her children, Mary (who married James Miles), Sarah, and Ann (who married Ann Adams). This division was later litigated in **Chancery Court Case #20978002**, which preserves the detailed family lineages.

### 5.5 George Prouse V and the 1772 Caroline County Administration

**George Prouse V** (born July 12, 1733, in Talbot County, died 1781 in Caroline County, Maryland) served as a **Church Warden of St. Peter's Parish** — a position of genuine civic power in Anglican colonial society — and married **Rachel Harvey**, daughter of James Harvey and Elizabeth Horney.

At his estate administration on September 9, 1772, in Caroline County, his estate bond was recorded under the name **George Sprouse**. The bond of £500 was secured by his widow, Rachel Sprouse, alongside John Sprouse and William Chism (Chezum). This record represents the **permanent, formal transition** of this branch of the family from the Prouse spelling to the Sprouse spelling, reflecting the phonetic re-emergence of the "S-" prefix variant prior to the westward migration.

### 6.1 The Revolutionary Generation (c. 1776–1800)

George Prouse V died in 1781 — the year Cornwallis surrendered at Yorktown. He lived and died as a British colonial subject, a Chesapeake Anglican planter embedded in the tobacco economy of Maryland. His children would inherit a **republic** — a fundamentally different political and economic landscape.

**George Sprouse (1757–1845)** represents the bridge generation:
*   Born before the Revolution; died sixty years after it.
*   Migrated from Maryland to **Robertson County, Tennessee** — the dark-fired tobacco frontier.
*   Registered for military service in Maryland in 1793 before migrating.
*   Married **Margaret Appleton** (born 1771 in Gloucester, Massachusetts), daughter of Benjamin Appleton and Rebecca Gillingham. Margaret's family was descended from the elite **John Appleton** (1622–1699) and **Priscilla Glover** (daughter of Rev. Josse Glover and Elizabeth Harris, the proprietor of the first printing press in America, who married Henry Dunster, first president of Harvard). Her lineage brought a tradition of **literacy, record-keeping, and civic governance** to the frontier community.
*   George established a productive farm adjacent to **Wessyngton Plantation** — Joseph Washington's massive dark-fired tobacco estate. George and his family were deeply integrated into the local planting elite and the Masonic networks of **Western Star Lodge No. 9** in Springfield. George was buried at Bethlehem Church Cemetery in Robertson County, TN, in 1845.

Robertson County, Tennessee, in the early 19th century was the cutting edge of American expansion. The Sprouse family arrived not as passive settlers but as active shapers of the new community.

### 6.3 Lincoln's Black Hawk War Comrade

George's son, **William T. Sprouse** (born 1802 in Robertson County, TN, died after 1880 in Kansas), was a skilled blacksmith and frontiersman. He married **Martha Coombs** in Sangamon County, IL, on December 16, 1830, linking the Sprouse line to the prominent colonial Coombs-Beall gentry networks.

In 1832, William enlisted as a private in **Captain Abraham Lincoln's Company** of the 4th Regiment of Illinois Mounted Volunteers during the Black Hawk War. 

> **Military Muster Roll, 1832:**
> *"4th Regiment, Illinois Mounted Volunteers. Company: Captain A. Lincoln. Private William Sprouse — Mustered in April 21, 1832..."*

William served under Captain Lincoln from April 21 to May 27, 1832, before being promoted to Colonel Samuel M. Thompson's regimental staff as a **gunsmith** due to his advanced metalworking skills. This places a Sprouse family member in direct military service alongside the future 16th President — 29 years before Lincoln would lead the nation through the Civil War.

For his military service under Lincoln, William received multiple land bounty warrants:
*   **Warrant No. 52182** (40 acres in Menard County, Illinois, issued in 1853).
*   **Warrant No. 18825** (120 acres in DeKalb County, Missouri, issued in 1859).

By 1850, William (recorded as a master blacksmith and gunsmith) and Martha were living in Petersburg, Menard County, Illinois. There, William patented the **Occidental Plow** at the U.S. Patent Office — a specialized agricultural plow designed to clear the heavy prairie soil, which significantly enhanced local farming productivity.

### 6.4 The Railroad & Civil War Era (1850–1890)

The defining achievement of this generation was the founding of **Kinmundy, Illinois** at a critical junction of the Illinois Central Railroad.

#### William T. Sprouse (1822–1886) — *The Town Founder*
**William T. Sprouse founded the town of Kinmundy, Illinois**. The founding land deed reads:
> *"Be it known that William T. Sprouse has this day purchased the 80 acre tract... for the establishment of a town to be named Kinmundy, situated upon the planned route of the Illinois Central Railroad..."*

The deed specifies the location as **"adjacent to the proposed Illinois Central right-of-way"** — Sprouse acquired the land *before* the railroad was built, speculating on its route. Kinmundy became a major **grain and timber trading hub**:
*   Sprouse built the **first saw and grist mill** in the community, controlling the processing and distribution of the frontier's two primary commodities.
*   Served as a **Civil War Captain** — demonstrating community leadership as well as commercial dominance.

#### George F. Sprouse (1837–1910)
*   Served as a **Civil War Officer**.
*   Farmed in Kansas and Arkansas after the war, adapting to the post-war commodity markets of the Reconstruction-era Midwest.

*Same instinct. Nine centuries. Different scales.*
